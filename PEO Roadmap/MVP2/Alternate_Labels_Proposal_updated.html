Summary and decision requested

Hold alternate labels and customer terminology in a governed lexicon layer beside the core vocabularies, not inside them. The core and domain assets keep preferred labels, definitions and relations only. Everything customers and Insperity call those concepts, and every concept only one customer uses, is captured, warehoused, mapped and managed in separate scoped graphs that point at the core by ID.

The result: onboarding a customer adds no triples to the core, a label change never forces a core release, one customer's terms never reach another, and every label traces to its evidence and the reviewer who approved it.

This is an evolving baseline. The same layer is meant to support configuration, customization, personalization, provisioning and insulation at scale, so every later project reuses it instead of rebuilding it.

The baseline is the set of semantic services. The review screens, provisioning, monitoring dashboards, triage and simulation are operational extensions of the target operating model, built on the services' contracts.

Decisions requested:

  1. Approve the lexicon-layer pattern: scoped lexicons for labels, customer extension vocabularies for new local concepts, and a derived resolution index.
  2. Approve the five-function service model (capture, warehouse, map, manage, serve) as the baseline for every customer and future project.
  3. Approve a benchmark spike before build, with pass thresholds set in advance, to prove the performance and release-stability claims.

02 of 19

Context and problem

The SKL is the single source of truth for vocabularies, taxonomies and ontologies, and publishes governed projections to execution points such as Visier. Customers describe the same things in their own words: their handbooks, documentation, CMS pages and data feeds carry plan names, abbreviations, job families and policy names.

Storing all of that as alternate labels on the core concepts fails in five ways:

  • Growth. The core grows with every customer, and so do its indexes and reasoning cost.
  • Churn. Every label change needs a core release and steward time.
  • Leakage. One customer's terms become visible to every other customer.
  • Ambiguity. The same word can mean different things to different customers, and a flat label list cannot say so.
  • Lost evidence. A bare label records neither where it came from, who approved it, nor when it applies.

Some customer terms are not synonyms at all. They name something the core has no concept for, and need a home that does not widen the core.

Design principles

Nine rules govern every choice below.

  1. The core holds meaning, not vocabulary variety. Core and domain assets carry preferred labels, definitions and relations, plus a small vetted set of universal alternate labels.
  2. A label is a record, not a property. Each label carries its scope, type, language, evidence, status, reviewer and dates.
  3. Scope is enforced by where a label lives. One named graph per tenant; no query path crosses tenants.
  4. References point one way. Lexicon and extension graphs point at core concepts by ID. Nothing is copied into the core. Every local concept also derives from a core or domain class, so it stays on a derivation path to the core; tenants mint no primitives.
  5. Machines propose, people decide. Extraction and matching create candidates; reviewers approve them. This follows the proposal's rule to automate the mechanical and govern the judgemental.
  6. Derived is not source. The resolution index is rebuilt from the graphs and never edited.
  7. Every change is traceable. Source, evidence span, reviewer, SKL release and publish record are kept for every label and mapping.
  8. One contract per function, an adapter per source or target. The design stays stack-independent and reversible.
  9. No direct access to a graph. Repositories are never exposed to people, other systems or third parties. Every read and write goes through a semantic service or a provisioned screen that logs it and applies the governance rules.

Architecture overview

Five functions share one warehouse. Capture brings content in, Map relates it to the core, Manage approves it and Serve delivers it. The warehouse holds every store, and the core and domain graphs are only read.

Read the top row as inputs, the middle row as the four functions in flow order, and the bottom band as the warehouse they share. Customer terms enter at Capture and leave through Serve. Nothing is written to the core and domain graphs by this flow; they change only through the council's release process.

The semantic services

The semantic services are the baseline. Every graph is reached only through them, and every later project, screen and dashboard is built on their contracts. Each service has one contract and, where it meets a source or a target, an adapter.

Group Service Responsibility
Inbound Ingestion Lands each item with tenant tag, provenance and change capture, through one connector per source type
Inbound Extraction Recovers text, structure and language, with OCR for scans
Inbound Screening Detects personal and sensitive content before any other step, and fails closed
Processing Candidates Extracts terms, defined terms and drafted definitions with evidence, then normalizes and merges variants
Processing Matching and triage Matches candidates to concepts, proposes match relations, and classifies each as a label, a local concept, a core request or a rejection
Processing Lexicon Writes label records and extension vocabularies to scoped graphs, validates their shapes and detects conflicts
Processing Review Holds the lifecycle state machine, assignments, decisions and the audit trail
Processing Release Runs validation, versioned releases, promotion and rollback across environments, and records changes as triples
Outbound Label resolution Answers tenant-scoped lookups from the derived index
Outbound Publishing Projects, validates, delivers and reconciles releases to targets through adapters
Common Policy decision Decides each request from persona, job step, resource and context
Common Telemetry Receives events from every service for monitoring, triage and simulation

Every service shares the same rules:

  • Each request carries a caller identity and a tenant context.
  • A policy decision is made and logged before any read or write.
  • Graph access is parameterized. No service accepts a raw query.
  • Each service emits events for request, decision, outcome and duration.
  • The contract is versioned, and changing it is a governed change.

Screens, provisioning, dashboards, triage and simulation are operational extensions of the target operating model. They call these services and never touch a graph or a repository.

Capture

Capture brings each customer's source material in once, tags it by tenant, screens it, and turns it into candidate terms with evidence. Four source types are in scope.

Source What it yields Intake
Customer documentation Defined terms, abbreviations, product and plan names Upload or file-share intake; version detection
Content management system Page titles, glossaries, taxonomies, navigation labels API or export; incremental sync; page-level provenance
Customer data feeds Code lists: job titles, departments, pay and benefit codes File, SFTP or API; declarative R2RML-style mappings to the candidate model
Employee handbooks Policy names, defined-terms sections, program and role names Section-aware parsing; OCR for scans

Every source passes the same five steps:

  1. Land. Store the raw item in the tenant's landing zone with tenant tag, source, version and hash.
  2. Screen. Detect personal data and sensitive content before anything else runs. The screen fails closed: unclear items are quarantined.
  3. Extract. Recover text and section structure, detect language, and run OCR with a confidence score on scans.
  4. Propose. Extract candidate terms, defined-term and glossary patterns, and abbreviations. A definition is drafted only when a source span supports it.
  5. Normalize. Collapse case, morphology and locale variants, and merge duplicates, keeping frequency and context.

The output is a candidate record: text, evidence span, source, tenant, frequency and confidence. Raw content stays in tenant-scoped storage. Only candidates move on.

The four sources feed one landing step; only the normalized candidate record leaves capture, and anything the screen cannot clear is held in quarantine.

Repositories and access

Assuming GraphDB, one server per environment (dev, test, stage, prod) hosts a few repositories. They are split by reasoning and release cadence, not by tenant.

Repository Holds Reasoning Why separate
Core and domain Core, domain and operational-domain vocabularies, taxonomies, ontologies and mapping graphs, one named graph per asset On, as the ontologies need Released and reasoned together
Lexicons The Insperity lexicon and one named graph per tenant lexicon Off or minimal Labels stay out of reasoning and out of core releases
Customer extensions Local concepts and their match relations, one named graph per tenant Light Own release and deletion per tenant

The raw landing zone, the candidate store and the resolution index need not live in GraphDB.

No repository is ever exposed to people, other systems or third parties. Each is reached only by a semantic service running under a service identity, and people work only through provisioned screens. Tenant isolation is therefore enforced by the services, with the named graph as the unit of scope. A repository per tenant, or per group of tenants, is the escalation path if a customer contract or a blast-radius concern requires it.

The core and domain repository is the same in every stage. Growth happens in the lexicon and extension repositories, and only where a contract or blast radius requires does a tenant move to a dedicated repository or server. Because graph names carry no tenant name, environment or repository, a move changes only the catalog record. In the diagram, t1, tn and t9 stand for tenant IDs.

The label record

A label is a resource in its own right, modeled in the style of SKOS-XL, with provenance terms from PROV-O and SHACL shapes that validate each record. This is what makes a label scopable, dateable and auditable without touching the concept it names.

Map

Map relates every candidate to the core and domain concepts, proposes where it should live, and flags anything ambiguous. It proposes; it never approves.

Matching. Lexical matching runs first, against preferred labels and existing approved labels after normalization. Embedding-assisted similarity runs second and always returns a confidence score. Each proposal carries a suggested match relation.

Match relations. A customer concept is linked to the nearest core or domain concept with one of four relations.

Relation Use when
Exact The customer concept means the same as the core concept
Close The meanings overlap but are not identical
Broad The core concept is wider than the customer concept
Narrow The core concept is more specific than the customer concept

A synonym, abbreviation or misspelling is not a match relation. It is a label on the concept it names. For example, an abbreviation for reduction in force becomes an abbreviation-type label on that concept.

Conflicts and homonyms. Map flags four cases and never resolves them on its own:

  • The same label points to different concepts in one scope.
  • A label equals another concept's preferred label.
  • The same label has different meanings in two tenants. Both stay scoped; the pair is flagged as evidence for the reviewers.
  • A candidate has no close match. It goes to triage as a possible local concept.

Extension vocabularies. A local concept has an ID, a definition, a status, at least one parent and its match relations. The parent is a core or domain class the concept derives from (a derivation edge), so every local concept stays on a path to the core. The match relation is the lateral link to a concept that names the same thing; it never replaces the parent. A tenant mints no new primitives: a concept that needs one goes to triage as a core change request. Concepts with several parents follow the proposal's multi-parent rule: one canonical concept, with fully qualified terms for each extra parent context. Derivation edges and match relations are re-checked on every core release, because a core change can leave either stale. A per-graph check reports any local concept with no derivation path to the core.

Data-feed codes map through declarative R2RML-style mappings into the same candidate model, so a code list and a handbook paragraph reach review in one form.

Manage

Manage turns proposals into approved, scoped content and keeps it healthy. Operational staff maintain labels, vocabularies, taxonomies, ontologies and mappings only through the provisioned screens below, within the scope their persona and job allow. The screens are an operational extension of the target operating model; the Review and Lexicon services beneath them are the baseline. Every edit is a proposal that is validated, reviewed, approved and released, never a direct write. A change beyond a persona's scope escalates to the next persona.

Triage

Each candidate is triaged into one of five destinations, with the evidence beside it.

The table below gives the same rules with the approver for each destination.

If the term is It goes to Approved by
The same meaning as an existing concept: a synonym, abbreviation, plural, locale variant or misspelling A label record in the narrowest lexicon in which it is true Ops reviewer
A homonym: the label already means something else in this scope Stays scoped, with the conflict flagged and a disambiguation note Domain steward
A new concept used by this customer only The customer extension vocabulary, as a concept derived from a core or domain class, with a match relation where it names the same thing Domain steward
A new concept of general value, or a label seen across several customers or in regulation A core or domain change request through the normal lifecycle Governance council
Noise, out of scope, or personal data Rejected with a reason; the evidence is kept Ops reviewer

Label lifecycle

A machine-proposed label starts as Proposed. A reviewer moves it to In review and then Approved, or Rejected. An approved label becomes Published only through a release, and a published label can later be Deprecated. Every transition records who, when and why.

Review and data-entry screens

Screen What operations do there
Candidate queue Filter by customer, source type, triage class, confidence and age; assign; watch the service-level timer
Evidence viewer See the source snippet with the term highlighted, the document or page, and other occurrences
Concept picker Choose among suggested matches with confidence, definition and hierarchy position
Alternate-label editor Set text, type, language, scope, effective dates and notes; accept, edit or reject
Conflict panel Resolve homonyms, clashes with preferred labels and cross-tenant duplicates
Local-concept creator Create an extension concept, its definition and its match relation
Lexicon browser Browse, search, edit, deprecate, and bulk import or export labels by scope
Escalation and promotion inbox Send core change requests to stewards; route label promotion to the council
Reviewer dashboard Track backlog, age of the oldest item, throughput, reviewer agreement and service levels

Promotion, demotion and releases

A label that recurs across customers, or appears in regulation, can be promoted to a domain or core vocabulary as a universal alternate label. The council approves it and it ships in a core release. A promoted label that later conflicts is demoted the same way. Lexicon changes release on their own cadence, per scope, through the same dev, test, stage and prod pipeline. A lexicon release never changes the core version. Decisions and releases are recorded as triples.

Serve

Serve makes approved terminology usable at run time and in downstream tools, without making the core carry it.

Label Resolution Service. Callers pass text and a tenant context. The service looks first in that customer's lexicon, then the Insperity lexicon, then the domain lexicon, then the core preferred labels. It never searches another tenant's graphs. Hidden labels match but are never displayed. If more than one concept matches at the same level, the service returns the candidates with their definitions so the caller can disambiguate. It answers from a derived index that is rebuilt on each release; it does not query the ontology graphs on the hot path.

Consumers. Search, Copilot, Compass, HRScale and partner APIs resolve terms through the same service, so one approved label behaves the same everywhere.

Publishing. The outbound services follow the proposal's contract: publish, validate, reconcile, status and rollback, with one adapter per target. For Visier, the proposal publishes released vocabularies and taxonomies as dimension members and property values, each stamped with its SKL ID and version. Synonyms and definitions stay in the SKL.

Customer terminology reaches Visier through a display-label rule, proposed here for confirmation: each member shows the tenant's approved label where one exists, and the preferred label otherwise. The full set of alternate labels never leaves the SKL. Whether Visier can hold a tenant-specific name for a published member is not yet confirmed. Task W6-01 asks Visier, and if the answer is no, the preferred label is published and customer terms resolve in Copilot, Compass and search instead.

The flow follows the publishing contract in the SKL proposal. Visier consumes and never edits; any difference found on read-back is drift and is corrected in the SKL.

Tenant isolation, security and privacy

Customer documents and terms are confidential to the customer. The design protects them in six ways.

  • No direct access. No repository is exposed to people, other systems or third parties. Graphs are reached only through the semantic services, and people work only through provisioned screens that log and audit every action and apply the governance rules. The store's own workbench and admin accounts are closed to normal use; any break-glass access is time-bound and logged.
  • Tenant tag from the first byte. Every item carries a tenant ID from landing onward, and every query carries a tenant context.
  • Isolation enforced by the services. Each tenant's lexicon and extension vocabulary is its own named graph, and the services build the graph restriction into every query from the caller's tenant context. Services accept no raw queries from callers or screens, and read and write use separate service accounts. Graph-level controls in the store, where available, are a second layer, not the first.
  • No cross-tenant path. There is no query that spans tenants. The one exception is promotion: a label recurring across customers is counted by the review service, which shows reviewers only the count, never another tenant's evidence.
  • Fail-closed screening. Personal and sensitive content is detected, redacted or quarantined before extraction. A red-team set tests it before the pilot gate.
  • Audit and exit. Every read and write is logged. Offboarding a customer removes its landing zone, graphs and index entries without any change to the core, which is a direct benefit of keeping its terms out of it.

Written permission to use each customer's documents and feeds, a retention rule and the data-use terms are conditions of onboarding, and are settled with security and legal in the first weeks of the plan.

Provisioning and operational personas

This section describes an operational extension of the target operating model, built on the semantic services. Access to every function is granted by persona and job, not by a list of who may touch which graph. The Personas & Jobs model will supply the fine-grained identity and access controls, which go beyond traditional ACLs.

Operational personas

Persona Jobs it performs Works on
Tenant provisioner Create, suspend and remove a tenant; rotate service credentials Tenant records and graphs, never content
Onboarding lead Register a customer's sources, confirm data permission, run onboarding One tenant's sources and ingest reports
Ops reviewer Decide candidates, edit labels, raise escalations Candidates and label records for assigned tenants
Domain steward Approve match relations and local concepts; maintain vocabularies, taxonomies and mappings Assets in the domains they steward
Ontologist Propose changes to ontologies, triage rules and naming Ontology assets, through the lifecycle
Platform operator Run pipelines, releases, rollback and drift response Services and releases, never content
Council member Approve releases, label promotion and cross-domain conflicts Release and promotion decisions
Auditor Read the audit trail and decision records Records only

The list is a proposal. The persona and job catalog is owned by Personas & Jobs, and each persona's jobs are broken into job steps that the screens and services present.

How access is decided

Each request carries four things: the person's persona assignments; the job and job step the screen or service call is performing; the resource, meaning graph class, tenant, domain and item state; and the context, such as assignment and time. The service makes the decision from rules authored in the SKL like other governed rules, and logs it with the persona, job and step that authorized it. These examples cannot be expressed as a list of permissions:

  • A reviewer can decide only items assigned to them, in tenants they are provisioned for, while the item is In review.
  • Nobody approves their own change: the persona that proposes cannot be the one that approves.
  • A steward's approval power ends at the domains they steward.
  • A person holding two personas has each persona's rights only while doing that persona's jobs.
  • Elevated access is time-bound, logged and reviewed.

Provisioning

Provisioning is itself a persona job. It runs through the same provisioned screens and under the same audit and governance rules.

  1. Provision a tenant. Register the tenant; create its lexicon and extension graphs, indexes and review queue; bind its policies and persona assignments; confirm written data permission; activate. Deprovisioning reverses each step and removes the graphs, raw store and index entries.
  2. Provision a person. Assign one or more personas with a tenant and domain scope and an end date; complete the persona's training and calibration; activate. Assignments are reassessed periodically, and a mover's or leaver's assignments are revoked at once.

Performance and scale

The core stays fast because customer growth happens elsewhere. Three design choices carry that claim:

  • Labels are annotations, not axioms, and live in graphs outside the ontology graphs, so reasoning never loads them.
  • Run-time lookup reads a derived index, not the graphs, so lookup cost does not depend on ontology size.
  • Each tenant is a separate graph, so loading, reindexing and deleting one customer touches no other.

These are claims until measured. Before the council approves the design, a benchmark spike (task W2-02) builds a synthetic core and holds the same labels two ways: in the core, and in scoped lexicons. It measures load time, reasoning time, query time, index size and release size at rising label volumes and tenant counts. Pass thresholds are agreed first (task W1-04) so the result cannot move them. The scale test in task W7-03 repeats the measurement on the built system.

Measure Target Tested in
Customer-driven triples added to the core per onboarding 0 Lexicon builder and second-customer onboarding
Core load and reasoning time at the top test volume Within a tolerance set before the spike Spike W2-02, scale test W7-03
P95 label lookup time Threshold set from the spike Spike W2-02, Label Resolution Service
Cross-tenant label results 0 Isolation tests
Core releases triggered by a label change None Release pipeline
Label records with source, reviewer and release 100% Review service

Customer counts and labels per customer are not yet known, so the spike uses synthetic parameters, which the decision record states. The design is revisited at the final gate with real volumes.

Options considered

Options B and C together, chosen by the triage rule, are recommended. Option A is kept only for a small vetted set of promoted labels.

Option How it works Core size and speed Tenant confidentiality Handles new customer meaning Verdict
A. Labels on core concepts Every synonym is an alternate label on the core concept Weak: grows with every customer Weak: all tenants see all labels Weak Reject for customer labels
B. Scoped lexicons Labels are records in one graph per scope, pointing at core concepts Strong: core unchanged Strong: scope is the graph Weak: names existing concepts only Adopt for label-only terms
C. Customer extension vocabularies New local concepts, each derived from a core or domain class, with match relations to the core Strong: core unchanged Strong Strong Adopt for new local concepts

Extension What it adds Calls Used by

Monitoring dashboards

Dashboards read telemetry, never the graphs. The measures listed above feed them.

  • Pipeline: volume by source type and tenant, failures, quarantined items, and lead time from source to candidate.
  • Processing quality: precision and recall on the gold set, candidate backlog, and the mix of triage classes.
  • Review work: queue size and age, throughput, reviewer agreement and service-level status.
  • Service health: latency and error rate for each service, including label lookup P95.
  • Access and security: denials, break-glass use, isolation test results, and persona assignments nearing expiry.
  • Releases and publishing: releases per environment, parity results, drift per release and rollback events.
  • Capacity: graph size, load and reasoning time by repository, and volumes per tenant, with trends.

Triage

Operational triage handles what goes wrong or needs attention. Alerts and exceptions from the dashboards enter one queue: failed ingests, quarantined items, drift found by reconcile, parity failures, repeated access denials, expiring assignments and service-level breaches. Each item has a severity, an owning persona and a runbook, and is assigned, worked and closed with an audit record. This is separate from the candidate triage in Manage, which decides where a term goes.

Simulation for planning and impact

Simulation shows what a change would do before it is made. A proposed change is loaded into a draft graph, and the simulation compares it with the current release without publishing anything. Simulation workspaces are graphs named with the reserved sim segment in the naming standard.

The changes worth simulating include:

  • a core or domain release;
  • promoting or demoting a label;
  • a changed match relation;
  • a new or changed access rule;
  • a new tenant, or growth in volume;
  • a changed projection to Visier, as a dry run.

The impact view shows the concepts, labels, mappings and tenants affected; the objects that would change in each target; new conflicts or homonyms; the reviewer workload created; and, for capacity runs, the predicted load, reasoning and lookup time. It is drawn as an impact graph with the change at the center and counts by tenant and target, so a council member can see the reach of a change before approving it.
